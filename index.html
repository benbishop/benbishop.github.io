Talkthrough

A lesson mode that gave learners a narrated, video style experience without anyone producing a video.

01
The observation

Skyllful customers train frontline workers on enterprise software, and many of them wanted an overview video to introduce a workflow before teaching it step by step.

Video is a reasonable instinct and a poor fit for this audience. Producing it takes time and skills most training teams do not have. The files are heavy, which matters when your learner is a delivery driver in a parking lot with two bars of signal. And when the software changes, when a button moves or a step is added or a customer name has to come out, you are reopening a video project you may not have organized well the first time.

So customers found their own way around it. They were exporting the assets we already generated, the voice over audio and the step screenshots, and assembling videos in Premiere or Camtasia themselves.

This was not hidden behavior. Our own documentation told them to do it. The Video authoring flow ended by instructing authors to download every screenshot and every audio file, then assemble the video in a tool of their choice.

02
The insight

If customers are building a workaround out of your own outputs, the workaround is the product.

03
What I designed

We already had everything the video needed: narration audio, step screenshots, and the step sequence. The only missing piece was playback.

Talkthrough assembles those existing assets into a narrated experience generated directly from the lesson. Press play, hear the narration, watch the workflow advance. No video editor, no export, no second artifact to keep in sync.

What it solved

ProblemHow Talkthrough addresses it
Production costGenerated from the lesson. No video tooling or video skills required.
File weightNarration and screenshots instead of streamed HD video, on field connections.
MaintenanceStep 7 changed? Update step 7. Not a video re-edit.
LocalizationFive languages was five video projects. Now it is one lesson, narrated per language.
Linear accessLearners jump straight to the step they need, mid job.

04
The authoring model

The design problem underneath was finding the smallest set of primitives that could express a narrated walkthrough. I settled on five actions an author composes per step, each carrying its own properties.

Action inspector

The five primitives, and what an author sets on each

NarrationSpeaks a script over the stepvoice, captions

Enough to describe almost any workflow, and few enough that an author can hold the whole model in their head. I put the authoring UI inside the existing Lesson Builder rather than in a separate tool, so the lesson stays the single source and the modes stay siblings.

Design to code, three generations

Seven years removing the same bottleneck, including the tool I built to remove it the first time.

The bottleneck

Skyllful's training simulations need pixel accurate replicas of enterprise app screens, because a field worker practicing on an approximation learns the approximation. Producing those replicas by hand was the constraint on everything. Someone designs a screen, then someone else rebuilds it in markup.

2019
Generation one

I built an Adobe XD plugin that exported artboards straight to production HTML. The interesting part was not the export. It was a C# object model of Adobe's scenegraph, faithful enough to carry gradients, boolean shape operations, repeat grids and symbol instances, and a rendering engine on the other side that emitted HTML, CSS, SVG and CSS keyframe animations, so exported screens animated rather than sat still.

The piece I am still fond of is a panel inside XD itself. Adobe XD had no concept of responsive anchoring that survived into code, so I added one, in the designer's own tool and the designer's own vocabulary. Check left, right, top, bottom on an element; the intent is stored back into the XD document as per element plugin data, travels with the file, and drives the CSS the service generates.

Days became hours.

Why it died

Two structural problems, neither of them technical. Customers had to buy and provision Adobe XD seats, which put a procurement conversation between a customer and their first lesson. And they had to rebuild production screens inside XD, duplicating work that reality had already done, because the real app was right there on the device.

Adobe abandoned XD later, which would have killed the tool anyway. We had already moved, for the adoption reasons, not the vendor one.

2021
Generation two

I prototyped a tool that takes a screenshot of the real application and reconstructs it as an editable replica. No design tool to license, and no screen to rebuild, because the input is something the customer already has and already sends us.

OCR gives you text and bounding boxes. Turning that into a button is the actual work: reasoning about the geometry around the text, what encloses it, what aligns with it, what the surrounding pixels look like.

The output stage was the previous generation's rendering engine, kept as a library. Two different ingestion paths, one renderer. Killing a product without throwing away the part of it that still worked.

Hours became minutes. Prototype in 2021, production in 2022.

2026
Generation three

The remaining prerequisite was that a human still had to capture and edit. So the third generation takes a video of someone doing the work and produces a finished lesson: transcription, frame extraction, vision based detection of the controls on screen, and a human review step where it matters.

The architectural bet was reconfigurability. The pipeline is a node graph defined as data rather than logic compiled into the app, so changing how lessons get built is an edit to a definition rather than a rewrite. I wrote the requirements document around that single hypothesis and cut self-service visual authoring out of scope to test it cleanly, which was the largest cut from earlier drafts and the right one.

I built it alone, directing coding agents, and handed the prototype to the engineering team, who took it into production development.

Click Catcher to heatmaps

An authoring shortcut in 2023 quietly created the data an analytics feature needed in 2025.

The shortcut

Authors building a practice screen had to define the interactive controls on it. In December 2023 I designed Click Catcher, which inverts that: draw only the control that is correct for this step, and every tap landing anywhere else records as incorrect. One region instead of many. It shipped as an authoring simplification, which is what it was.

The by-product

To record a tap as incorrect, the system has to record where it landed. So from that release onward the platform accumulated tap coordinates for every wrong answer, on every step, from every learner. Nobody scoped that as a data collection feature. It fell out of a decision made for a different reason.

What it became

Two years later I designed the Lessons Dashboard and step heatmaps on top of it. A grid of lessons against steps, colored by failure rate. Click a red cell and you get the screen replica the learner saw, the instruction they were given, a green outline around the control that was correct, and dots showing where people actually tapped.

When the dots cluster somewhere that is not the green outline, you are not looking at learners who failed. You are looking at an instruction that pointed at the wrong thing.

The point

Most training analytics tell you a learner failed. This tells you which sentence to rewrite.

NOV Remote

A phone replacing a physical remote control for industrial drilling equipment. I designed it and wrote all of it.

Screens

Designed for
the environment

The app pairs over Bluetooth with an equipment controller, sends it operating codes, and shows live status back: motor voltage limits, restart countdowns, connection state.

Every visual decision here comes from where it gets used rather than from taste. Dark ground with white text, readable in direct sun and not destructive to night vision on a rig after dark. Numerals large enough to read at arm's length while your attention is mostly somewhere else. Values zero padded, so 012s and 030V hold their width instead of shifting as they change. Portrait locked, deliberately, because the other hand is doing something.

Connection state and signal strength sit in the header on every screen, because with Bluetooth the first question is always whether you are connected to the right thing.

The detail that gives away real field work is the flashlight in the footer. The app assumes it will be used in the dark, so rather than sending you out to the operating system for a torch, it puts one a tap away from the controls.

A safety boundary
in the interface

Some commands can damage equipment or endanger people, so those are gated. The device displays a number, the operator calls NOV Australia Service and quotes it, and enters the code they are given back.

A shared password would have been easier and worse. A challenge and response makes authorization per device, revocable in practice, and puts a human at NOV in the loop at the moment it matters.

Underneath

Cross platform for iOS and Android, MVVM, Bluetooth Low Energy. The hard parts of BLE on mobile are not the protocol, they are the platform edge cases: runtime permissions, devices that do not support BLE at all, and releasing the connection when the app goes to the background, because a phone quietly holding a controller in someone's pocket locks everyone else out of it.

Data Review Tool

Customers whose own data policies stopped them using the product at all.

The blocker

The screenshot workflow has an awkward property: the input is a picture of the customer's real software, and real software is full of real records. Patient names, student records, account numbers, whatever that customer's business is made of.

For customers operating under HIPAA, GDPR or FERPA, sending those images to a vendor's server was not a preference they could be talked out of. It was policy. Some of them could not adopt the feature at all.

The move

Redaction moves to before the upload rather than after it. Screenshots are held in browser memory, text recognition runs locally, and pattern based rules, custom expressions and CSV driven rule sets mark what has to go. Nothing reaches our servers until a person has looked at each image and approved it.

Why the boundary
is the design

You can build an excellent redaction tool that runs on your own servers, and it does not solve this problem, because for these customers the violation already happened at upload. Moving the work across the boundary is the entire idea. Everything else is implementation.

It unblocked customers who had been prevented by their own data policies from adopting the screenshot workflow at all. It landed the same year I served as one of two internal leads on the company's first SOC 2 Type II certification, owning the platform and product security architecture, which is the other half of the same story: making an enterprise product that a regulated buyer's security review can actually clear.
